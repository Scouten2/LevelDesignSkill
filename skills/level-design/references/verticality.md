## 1. Stance: height is a primary design axis

Most levels are designed in plan and get height as decoration or as a sniper perch. Treat height as one of the most powerful and least used tools you have: it adds routes, previews, secrets, shortcuts and emotion without adding footprint. Design every level in **section as well as plan**. TLDB defines verticality as "vertical flow, how it feels to move upwards and downwards", and notes that up and down are "much more obvious and unmistakable" goals than compass directions. Taylor's first principle already asks for interest "through verticality, secondary paths, hidden areas".

Load this file whenever a space has two or more floor planes, a drop, a climb, a lift, a vista, or the brief mentions exploration, secrets, Souls-like or immersive-sim structure. The eight-function list and the hidden-but-fair rule are a synthesis of the sources cited here [convention].

## 2. The eight functions of height

For every vertical feature, name which function(s) it serves. A height change that serves none is noise; merge it into one floor (TLDB).

**2.1 Preview / survey (look down, then plan).** Put a high vantage before each major space so players read its layout, routes and threats before committing. TotK's sky dive is "a tool for gathering information about the surface by surveying it from above" (Fujibayashi); Dohta adds that players hesitate to dive onto unfamiliar ground, so surveys work best over places that are partly known. TLDB: a vista previews landmarks and "an arena layout before an encounter begins". Elden Ring previews the Lake of Rot from an Ainsel River side path, as Dark Souls showed the Demon Ruins from a Tomb of the Giants ledge. *Check:* what the survey shows is actually reachable, by a route the player can roughly guess.

**2.2 Aspiration (look up at the goal).** Put the goal above the player and keep it in view as they climb. Portal 2's "The Ascent" compresses the player in a cave, then draws the eye upward through a chain (elevator tower, sparking cables, falling debris, test spheres receding upward) to give "a visual sense of the daunting climb". *How:* never rely on players looking up unprompted; chain two or three moving or lit cues upward. A spiral path keeps the goal overhead at each turn. Celeste uses the summit as its spine.

**2.3 Memory / loopback (look down at where you've been, then return).** From each high point, frame an earlier space; later, reconnect to it by lift, ladder or drop that lands at a recognisable landmark. Dark Souls' Undead Parish lift drops the player into Firelink Shrine, revealing they were above the hub all along. *Check:* each dangerous vertical excursion ends with a vertical shortcut to safety.

**2.4 Cheap commitment (drops as valves).** "With gravity, it is easier to drop down than to climb up. Downward flow is heavier than upward flow" (TLDB). A drop says everything behind it is done; shooters use it to force an encounter. Climbs cost more: stairs, ramps and ladders need reserved space, and lifts cost scripting. *How:* commit with drops, return with ladders and lifts; make every drop read as one-way before the player steps off.

**2.5 Footprint reuse (stack, invert, re-traverse).** Play one column twice. Elden Ring's Carian Study Hall "inverts its entire structure", so a climb becomes "a descent". The Divine Tower of Caelid turns pediments into ramps and makes the interior a descent puzzle where "even a sconce may serve as a platform". Titanfall 2's "Effect and Cause" stacks two copies of a level. Hitman's spirals "feel bigger than they really are". *How:* second pass in the opposite vertical direction, with a modifier (Taylor's bi-directional reuse); windows between stacked routes so each previews the other.

**2.6 Hidden routes (look over the edge, or up the wall).** A lower roof, a ledge chain down a cliff, a beam across a shaft, a ladder behind a wall. Stormveil's lower rooftops; Shadow of the Erdtree made a reviewer "peep over cliffsides for hidden ledges"; Elden Ring's Hero's Graves have "a lower precarious section, accessible by dropping from a much higher ledge". These reward curiosity only if they are fair: apply §4.

**2.7 Emotional meaning (ascent vs descent).** Ascent reads as effort, hope, aspiration, escape; descent as dread, isolation, distance from safety. Dark Souls: "the further you plunge, the further from the safety of the central hub you get" (TheGamer, opinion). Taylor: windows below knee height feel powerful and voyeuristic, above shoulder height persecuting. Fall cost changes meaning: Celeste's instant respawn makes height pure aspiration; Getting Over It and Jump King make height a store of loss, so dread and relief come from falling. *How:* match the level's net vertical direction to its emotional arc; put the most dangerous encounter at the deepest point of a descent [convention]; make the climb out read as relief.

**2.8 Combat and stealth position.** High ground sees more and is hit less. Give every perch a counter: a second perch, a flank, an exposed route up. DOOM 2016 arenas loop around structures for regaining height. Quake 3's "The Longest Yard" uses jump pads for strong upward flow, and a far sniper platform counters the predictable flight arcs (TLDB). Counter-Strike stays "relatively flat" for low-sensitivity aim and gets situational height from boosting. Stealth overlooks let players plan routes.

## 3. See it, then reach it from an unexpected height

FromSoftware's signature vertical move: show a place first, at a distance or below; later deliver the player into it from a height or direction they did not expect. Seeing it plants an expectation; arriving pays it off and rebuilds the player's mental map in one beat.
- **Dark Souls:** Blighttown "can be seen from Firelink Shrine itself below the cliff"; the player later arrives at it from above after a long descent [unverified for the route]. The Undead Parish lift drops into Firelink, revealing the player was above the hub all along; another lift descends to New Londo.
- **Stormveil Castle:** the first quarter teaches looking up with items and birds overhead (JM8, The Escapist). Three ways in: the front gate under ballista fire, Gostoc's side path, and a ladder route [unverified]. The lower rooftops below the walls are the hidden layer.
- **Liurnia's cliffs** [unverified]: the lake basin and Raya Lucaria Academy are first seen from the cliffs above; the stepped cliff rim hides ledges, caves and items reached by dropping ledge to ledge.
- **Siofra and Ainsel:** whole underground regions under the surface map. Siofra's lift is "in plain sight", trading the surprise of Dark Souls' illusory-wall Great Hollow for accessibility; the Lake of Rot is previewed from Ainsel before it is reached.
- **Carian Study Hall and the Divine Tower of Caelid:** the same space reached from the other end (§2.5).
- **Shadow of the Erdtree:** Miyazaki aimed for "not quite open field and not quite legacy dungeon… that better sense of verticality". Areas stack; some are reached only by dropping from far above or climbing from far below.

**How to build one:** (1) choose the destination; (2) give it a vantage from which it is clearly visible but clearly not reachable *yet*; (3) route the player away; (4) bring them back above or below it by a route they did not see as related; (5) put a landmark at the arrival so the recognition lands; (6) open a shortcut from it to a safe point.

## 4. The hidden-but-fair vertical route

A hidden vertical route is fair when all four hold [convention, synthesis]:
1. **Grammar taught.** The player has already seen a drop or climb of this type succeed, in a safe, low-stakes place (Stormveil taught looking up before relying on it).
2. **Visible from another vantage.** The route can be seen from somewhere (across the gap, from below, from a lift), even if not from the entry point. This is "see it, then reach it".
3. **Edge invitation.** Something rewards a look over the edge or up the wall: a glint, a corpse or item, light from below, an enemy patrolling beneath, a message.
4. **Low cost of trying.** The drop is free or committed (§5) and a checkpoint is near, so curiosity is not punished with lost progress.

Optional content may skip rule 2. **Critical-path content may not**, and must not be the only way forward unless rules 1–4 all hold and a playtest confirms discovery.

## 5. Drops and climbs: bands from your metrics

There is no industry-standard safe-fall height; derive bands from the game's own jump apex, mantle reach and damage thresholds, and keep them game-wide [convention].

| Band | Definition (from metrics) | Use for | Signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Free** | ≤ height the player can get back up (jump apex + mantle) | Casual connections, exploration | Low ledge, visible floor |
| **Committed** | Above free, below the damage threshold: safe, no way back here | Valves, encounter commits, hidden-route entries | Visible lit landing within downward pitch; earlier safe examples; prop bridges (awning, cart, lower roof) |
| **Costly** | Damage threshold to lethal threshold | Optional shortcuts with a visible reward | Visible landing plus a hint of cost (debris, a corpse) |
| **Lethal** | Beyond the lethal threshold, or void | Boundaries | No visible bottom (fog, darkness), distinct ground material, objects or enemies seen dying there, or a barrier |

**Converting thresholds:** engines define fall damage by distance or by impact speed. Height for an impact speed is **h = v² / 2g**, with g the game's effective gravity (base × gravity scale; jump math in `metrics.md` §3).
- Real gravity (9.81 m/s²): 10 m/s → 5.1 m; 14 m/s → 10.0 m; 20 m/s → 20.4 m.
- Example kit (take-off 700 cm/s, gravity scale 1.75, g = 1715 cm/s²; values illustrative): apex = 700²/3430 ≈ 143 cm; with a 200 cm mantle reach, free ≤ 2.0 m. If damage starts at 1200 cm/s: 1200²/3430 ≈ 4.2 m. If lethal at 2000 cm/s: 2000²/3430 ≈ 11.7 m. Bands: free ≤ 2.0 m, committed 2.0–4.2 m, costly 4.2–11.7 m, lethal above. With a 4 m storey, one storey is committed and three are costly.
- If fall speed is capped (terminal velocity v_t), every fall longer than v_t²/2g lands at v_t; check which model the engine uses before trusting a height.
- 2D games without fall damage collapse to free / committed / lethal (pits); camera locks can also commit.

**Lookalike rule.** Drops that look alike (similar height, same landing material) must behave alike. Keep every authored drop out of a dead zone around each threshold (about ±10% [convention]), as Schatz buffers ledge heights, so no drop is "maybe survivable". **Climbs** use the game's affordance language, are visible from below before they are needed, and respect the ledge dead zones in `metrics.md`.

## 6. Limits and costs

- **About three floor planes per area.** "Most maps tend to max-out at three different floor planes for any given area… a fourth (p)lane doesn't add new dynamics"; merge minor height changes into one floor (TLDB).
- **Gamepad camera pitch.** Console players "often park their camera's vertical rotation" at head or chest height; "high frequency height variation" frustrates them; flying enemies should keep a stable height (TLDB).
- **Structure for 3D connectivity:** Hitman's snail-house spirals and floors players can name; Titanfall 2's "window pane" lanes (`layout-and-flow.md` §3). Without one, 3D connectivity "becomes a mess" (Fukada).
- **Upward flow costs space** (stairs and ramps need room) and lifts cost scripting (TLDB).
- **AI** needs nav links for every drop, ladder and jump it should use; otherwise perches become exploits or enemies teleport [convention].
- **Performance:** long downward views into stacked spaces defeat occlusion and streaming; flag them for tech review [convention].

## 7. Failure modes and fixes

| Failure | Evidence | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden route never found; level feels "incredibly linear and punishing" | Stormveil, for players who did not realise "they could jump off the ledge and navigate the castle's lower rooftops" (JM8 via Wikipedia) | §4: teach the drop safely first, show the roof from another vantage, invite the look |
| Stacked layers without layered map; players have "no idea how to get to a certain area" | Shadow of the Erdtree: no underground toggle; a map fragment "locked in a ravine deep below the zone" (eXputer, opinion) | Layer toggle or diegetic equivalent; markers say which layer; access points visible from the layer above |
| Required cue overhead, missed | Valve: "Players very rarely look up" (Portal 2, Game Informer 2010); testers stuck until a single hole was moved "down to the eye level of the player" | Required cues within normal pitch; optional content may sit above or below |
| Drop danger unreadable | Player and developer complaints about unclear survivable heights [unverified, anecdotal] | §5 bands, lookalike rule, consistent landing materials |
| Punishing fall erases too much progress | Getting Over It bounds loss with "outcroppings designed for you to hook onto when falling" | Catch ledges and hard-to-fall-out-of rests at intervals; state the fall-cost policy |
| Art pass hides vertical cues | Props and lighting break drop signals and look-up leads first | Re-check every vertical cue after art (`process.md`) |

## 9. Verticality by genre

- **Platformers:** height is the progress bar (Celeste's mountain); look ahead upward in climbs; choose the fall-cost policy as deliberately as gap size (`platformer-camera.md`, `platformer-metrics.md`).
- **Metroidvanias and dungeons:** vertical shafts as hubs that later reconnect by shortcut [convention]; drops as one-way valves with the return shown (`genre-dungeon-metroidvania.md`).
- **Immersive sims and stealth:** roofs, vents, balconies and windows as alternate routes; Hitman's spirals; high places as safe observation [convention] (`genre-stealth-immersive-sim.md`).
- **Horror:** descent as dread (lower ceilings, less light, further from safety), climbing out as relief [convention] (`genre-horror.md`).
- **Multiplayer:** every high ground has a counter; three planes max; flat or gentle slopes for gamepad aim; lanes for 3D movement (`genre-multiplayer.md`).
- **Open worlds:** survey points and towers before regions; layered-map support (`genre-open-world.md`).
- **Layered worlds (TotK):** sky, surface and Depths are three stacked layers, with the dive as the survey tool; the Depths mirror surface features [unverified].

## Review checklist

The Z-axis check. Run it on every level with height in it.

- [ ] **Section drawn** for every multi-level area, floor planes labelled; ≤3 floor planes per area
- [ ] **Look-up moment:** the player sees the goal above, led by a chain of upward cues (light, motion, structure)
- [ ] **Look-down moment:** a survey vantage over the next (or a later) space before committing
- [ ] **Look-back moment:** an earlier area seen from a new height, ideally paid off by a vertical shortcut
- [ ] **Vertical loop:** the far end of each dangerous stretch connects back by lift, ladder or drop to a known safe point
- [ ] **Hidden vertical route** passes the four-part rule (grammar taught, visible from another vantage, edge invitation, low cost); critical-path routes never skip visibility
- [ ] **Critical info within camera pitch,** or explicitly led to
- [ ] **Every drop banded** free / committed / costly / lethal from the game's metrics; lookalike drops behave alike; no drop in a threshold dead zone
- [ ] **Climbs legible:** affordance language, visible from below before needed, still legible after the art pass
- [ ] **One-way reads as one-way** before commitment
- [ ] **Layer support:** stacked regions shown on the map (or diegetic equivalent); markers say which layer
- [ ] **Camera and input fit:** no high-frequency height changes for gamepad aim; vertical camera dead zone for third person; flying enemies at stable heights
- [ ] **AI nav links** authored for every vertical connection enemies should use; deliberate escape gaps noted
- [ ] **Fall-cost policy stated** (respawn, damage, lost progress); catch geometry on punishing climbs
- [ ] **Emotional direction:** net vertical direction matches the intended emotion; the deepest point is the most dangerous
- [ ] **Performance:** long downward views into stacked spaces flagged for occlusion and streaming review
